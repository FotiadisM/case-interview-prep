# Competitive Response Cases (Extended)

A competitive response case asks: a competitor has done something (cut prices, launched a product, entered your market, acquired a rival) — how should the client react? Or the mirror image: the client is about to move, and how will competitors respond? These are game-thinking cases: the answer is never just "match them or don't."

Source: [PrepLounge – Competitive response basics](https://www.preplounge.com/en/case-interview-basics/competitive-response), [CaseRoomPrep – Competitive response framework](https://www.caseroomprep.com/case-interview-prep/competitive-response-framework), [HackingTheCaseInterview – Competitive strategy](https://www.hackingthecaseinterview.com/pages/competitive-strategy-case-interview)

## The core structure: assess → predict → choose → execute

**1. Understand the threat.**
- What exactly did the competitor do, in which segments, with which customers?
- Is it credible and sustainable? (Do they have the cost structure / capacity / will to sustain a price war, or is this a promotional blip?)
- What does it do to our economics — quantify the exposure: revenue at risk by segment.

**2. Diagnose the client's position.**
- Where do we make money (intersection of customers, products, geographies)? The threat matters only where our profit pools are.
- What are the client's advantages (brand, switching costs, cost position, distribution, lock-in)?
- What are the competitor's actual weaknesses (service, breadth, balance sheet)? Counter-attacks should target competitor weaknesses, not mirror their moves.

**3. Generate and evaluate options** across the full menu, then screen:
- Match price / undercut — cheap immediacy, dangerous escalation.
- Do nothing — viable only if exposure is small or the move is unsustainable.
- Differentiate / re-position — raise value instead of lowering price; reinforce switching costs.
- Counter-attack — strike their profit pool (in their home market/segment).
- Cooperate / de-escalate — signaling to discourage price wars (public pricing policy, capacity restraint).
- Redraw the game — strategic move (channel, product architecture, partnerships) that changes the rules.

Screen each option on: impact (revenue/margin at stake), cost, speed, competitor's likely response, and risk. Game theory in plain language: *what do we do, and what do we estimate they'll do in response?*

**4. Recommend proportionately.** The response should match the threat's size and credibility — overreacting validates a blip; underreacting gifts share. Give the trigger conditions for escalation and the stop signal to de-escalate.

## Key factors and considerations

- **Sustainability test first** — most "threats" in cases are actually unsustainable (high-cost entrant, funding that won't last, price below their own cost). Quantify how long the competitor can bleed.
- **Profit-pool focus** — respond where you earn profit, not where the drama is.
- **Best-response thinking** — interviewers reward naming your action, their probable reaction, and your follow-on ("we match in segment A only; they can't follow without hurting segment B where they're weakest").
- **Commitment and signaling** — credible deterrence (visible capacity, long-term contracts) can prevent the war before it starts.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Mirroring automatically** — price matching without testing their sustainability and your asymmetry.
2. **Treating it as pure strategy with no numbers** — quantify the exposure and the cost of each option.
3. **Ignoring retaliation loops** — the response must include the anticipated counter-response.
4. **One-dimensional menu** — only price responses; the strongest answers redraw the game (flank their weakness).

## Practice prompts

- Low-cost carrier cuts prices 40% on your profitable hub routes. Respond.
- A Thai sugar exporter expands capacity, threatening to drop Indonesian prices (PrepLounge "Sugar to Indonesia" case). Advise the Indian client.
- A tech giant launches a free version of your core product. Now what?
- Your channel partner starts selling a competing private label. Respond.
- A venture-funded entrant undercuts you by 30% in one metro market. Is it sustainable, and what do you do?
