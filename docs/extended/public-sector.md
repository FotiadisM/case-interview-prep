# Public Sector & Social Impact Cases (Extended)

Government, nonprofit, and social-sector cases are asked by McKinsey (which has the largest public-sector practice of the Big 3), BCG, Bain's social-impact arms — and by dedicated firms like Bridgespan and Dalberg. The interview format is identical; the objective function, metrics, and stakeholder landscape are not.

Source: [HackingTheCaseInterview – Public sector guide](https://www.hackingthecaseinterview.com/pages/public-sector-consulting-case-interview), [Road to Offer – Government cases](https://www.roadtooffer.com/blog/government-public-sector-case-interview), [HackingTheCaseInterview – Social impact](https://www.hackingthecaseinterview.com/pages/social-impact-case-interview)

## How it differs from a corporate case

| Dimension | Private sector | Public / social sector |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Objective | Max profit & shareholder value | Max citizen outcomes / public value |
| Success metrics | Revenue, margin, ROI, share | Cost per citizen served, adoption, social outcomes |
| Stakeholders | CEO, board, investors | Agencies, legislators, citizens, unions, oversight bodies, donors |
| Constraints | Competition, market forces | Budgets, regulation, political feasibility |
| Timeframes | Quarterly cycles | Annual / multi-year budget cycles |

The two big traps: applying a profit framework, and ignoring **political feasibility** — in public-sector cases the highest-impact option may be unimplementable, and a recommendation that ignores that fails the case.

## The core framework: the Public Value test

Evaluate any public program/policy across three tests (Moore's Public Value Triangle, per Road to Offer):

1. **Public value** — does it produce outcomes citizens actually care about (health, education, safety, equity)?
2. **Legitimacy & support** — do the stakeholders with power (political sponsors, agencies, media, unions, taxpayers) support it?
3. **Operational feasibility** — can it be delivered with available budget, staff, and timeline?

A recommendation must pass all three. Supporting frameworks: **PESTEL** (political, economic, social, technological, environmental, legal) for the environment; **stakeholder mapping** (influence × alignment) to find allies and blockers; **impact maximization** (reach × depth × cost per beneficiary) for allocation decisions; **policy analysis** (objectives → stakeholders → implementation → expected outcomes incl. unintended consequences) for should-we-do-policy-X questions.

## Step-by-step approach

1. **Understand the mandate and mission** — what change is the client trying to create, and who are the beneficiaries?
2. **Clarify how success is measured** — the most important question ("How does the client define success?"). In ~40% of social-impact cases the success metric differs from what the prompt suggests.
3. **Structure** — pick the lens that matches the problem (allocation → impact maximization; policy → policy analysis; delivery → public value triangle + operations).
4. **Analyze** — quantify with social-sector math: cost per beneficiary, program reach (population × eligibility × take-up), funding gap (cost of program − committed funding), deadweight (beneficiaries who'd have gotten the outcome anyway).
5. **Test feasibility** — political, fiscal, operational. Map stakeholders: who can kill this, who can carry it, who must be engaged?
6. **Recommend** — tied to the impact metric, with a clear theory of change, risks, and the stakeholder sequence for getting it through.

## Quantifying impact: Social ROI (workhorse math)

Monetize outcomes to compare options when there's no P&L. Example (Road to Offer's early-childhood case): one cohort of a childcare program monetizes graduation improvements, special-education savings, and justice savings into a 1.7x social ROI — build such a bridge: program cost → outcomes by type → dollar value per outcome (from research/benchmarks) → ratio. Coin the assumptions.

## Key factors and considerations

- **Outputs vs outcomes** — outputs are activities (1,000 children enrolled), outcomes are changes (reading proficiency +15 pts). Interviewers test whether you know the difference.
- **Equity dimension** — who benefits and who doesn't matters in social cases the way margin matters in corporate ones.
- **Funding structure** — restricted vs unrestricted grants, donor concentration, budget-cycle timing all constrain options.
- **Politics** — elections, incumbent incentives, union power: model them as constraints, not noise.
- **Partnerships** — public-private-philanthropic collaboration is often the realistic delivery mechanism.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Defaulting to a profit framework** — the single biggest mistake; ask the impact metric first.
2. **Recommending the ideal without the feasible** — the public-value triangle exists because value without support dies.
3. **Ignoring stakeholders** — government cases are overdetermined by actors with veto power; map them early.
4. **Soft-balling the math** — social cases still demand quantified reach, cost per outcome, and funding gaps.
5. **Mistaking outputs for outcomes** — 1,000 flyers distributed ≠ behavior changed.

## Practice prompts

- A city wants to cut homelessness 30% in five years. How?
- A foundation must allocate $100M across education programs. Structure the decision.
- A health ministry wants to double vaccination uptake in rural areas.
- A national job-training agency has 50% drop-out rates. Diagnose and fix.
- Evaluate: should a government subsidize EV adoption?
