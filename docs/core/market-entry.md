# Market Entry Cases

Among the most frequent prompts at all three firms: "Should our client enter market X — and if so, with which strategy?" It usually runs with a market-sizing component built in. Market entry is frequently the destination of other case types too — a growth case or a new product case often resolves into "should we enter this market and how?"

Source: [HackingTheCaseInterview – case types](https://www.hackingthecaseinterview.com/pages/case-interview-types), [IGotAnOffer – Market entry case](https://igotanoffer.com/blogs/mckinsey-case-interview-blog/market-entry-case), [Road to Offer – case types](https://www.roadtooffer.com/blog/case-interview-types)

## What the case is asking

The three variants:

1. **Geographic expansion** — existing product, new country/region (the classic).
2. **Adjacent market / new customer segment** — same core capability, new product-market combination.
3. **New channel** — e.g., a wholesaler going direct-to-consumer.

The trap: treating "should we enter?" as a binary question. **The real question is "should we enter, and if so, how and when?"** (Road to Offer). Answering the *how* (build / buy / partner) is what separates passing from strong.

## The core framework

Four statements all need to hold — structure your analysis around testing each:

1. **Market is attractive** — size, growth, profitability, competitive intensity, trends (regulatory, technological).
2. **We can compete** (competition weak or beatable) — who's there, their position, barriers to entry, our differentiation.
3. **We have the capabilities** — what must be true to win (brand, distribution, local knowledge, talent, capex) and does the client have or can build it?
4. **The economics work** — investment required, expected margin, payback, breakeven, and risk-adjusted return.

Then a fifth, decision-quality layer: **entry mode** — build (greenfield), buy (acquisition), or partner (JV / franchise / distribution deal), matched to speed requirements, capital appetite, risk tolerance, and local requirements (e.g., regulatory constraints forcing a partner).

## Step-by-step approach

1. **Restate and clarify** — which market, with what product, what's the objective (share, profit, learning), and what time horizon?
2. **Size the market first** (top-down and bottom-up; sanity-check against each other) — you need an order of magnitude before "attractive" means anything.
3. **Assess attractiveness** — growth and profitability matter more than raw size; check trends and lifecycle stage.
4. **Analyze competition** — structure (fragmented vs concentrated), economics of incumbents, switching costs, barriers to entry. A common failure: spending 15 minutes sizing and never reaching competitive analysis. Interviewers often care more about competitive insight than the exact market size.
5. **Test capabilities** — honestly map requirement vs possession; identify gaps and how they'd be closed.
6. **Quantify the economics** — revenue potential (market size × realistic share), capex/launch costs, margin profile, breakeven timing, payback.
7. **Decide the entry mode** — trade off speed vs cost vs control vs risk; joint ventures share profit but cut risk; acquisitions are fast but expensive and integration-risky; organic builds are cheap but slow.
8. **Recommend conditionally** — "Enter country X via a JV with a local distributor, contingent on..." — plus risks (competitor response, macro/political, execution) and sequenced next steps (pilot city, then scale).

## Key factors and questions to consider

- **Market size vs. share reality** — a big market with entrenched leaders and high switching costs can be worse than a smaller fragmented one.
- **Adjacency advantage** — do the client's existing assets (brand, supply chain, customer base) transfer? Non-transferable advantage is the biggest hidden killer of entries.
- **Cannibalization** — will the entry steal share from the client's existing business?
- **Regulatory and local barriers** — local content rules, licensing, tariffs; these can force a partnership.
- **Competitor response** — assume incumbents react (price wars, bundled retaliation); model the response.
- **Entry cost asymmetry** — sunk costs, minimum efficient scale, and staged (reversible) entry options.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Treating it as binary** — missing the "how and when."
2. **Boiling the ocean on market sizing** and never reaching competition/capabilities/entry mode.
3. **Ignoring capabilities fit** — attractive markets the client can't win are worthless.
4. **Skipping entry mode entirely**, or defaulting to the cheapest/most expensive option without a trade-off analysis.
5. **Forgetting competitor response and exit costs** — entry is a multi-period game.
6. **Recommending "enter" or "don't enter" without conditions** — great answers are conditional and sequencing-aware (pilot first, options to scale or exit).

## Worked example (condensed)

**Prompt:** A European fashion retailer wants to enter Southeast Asia. Should they launch in Thailand or Vietnam first?

**Structure:** "I'll evaluate (1) market attractiveness of both countries, (2) competitive landscape, (3) our capabilities and transferability, (4) economics of each option, and (5) entry mode."

**Analysis (skeleton):** Thailand's apparel market is larger but consolidated by local incumbents with strong malls real estate; Vietnam is smaller but growing faster, younger, more fragmented, and has a fast-rising middle class. Capability gaps: local sourcing and logistics network exist in neither; brand awareness is low in both.

**Quantify:** Sizing: Vietnam apparel ≈ $X, capturing 5% by year 3 ≈ $Y revenue at Z% margin → breakeven in 3.5 years via a JV with a local retail group vs 5 years greenfield.

**Recommendation:** "Enter Vietnam first via a JV with a local retail partner. Vietnam offers faster growth and a fragmented competitive set; the JV de-risks real-estate and regulatory learning. Thailand remains attractive later once the regional playbook is proven. Key risk is local-partner conflict; I'd structure the JV with clear exit clauses and stage the rollout city by city."

## Practice prompts

- A US beer company considers entering the hard-seltzer market. Assess.
- A ride-hailing app wants to enter a Southeast Asian country. Should they?
- A high-end gym chain considers a budget brand for students. Go or no-go?
- A German B2B software firm considers the US market. How would you advise?
- PE-owned airline considers launching long-haul low-cost routes. Evaluate.
