# Growth Strategy Cases

A growth strategy case asks how a company should grow revenue, profit, or market share — typically over a 3–5 year horizon. Together with profitability and market entry, growth is one of the three most common archetypes at MBB. Cases regularly mutate: a growth case may turn into market entry, pricing, or an acquisition question partway through.

Source: [HackingTheCaseInterview – Growth strategy](https://www.hackingthecaseinterview.com/pages/growth-strategy-case-interview), [Road to Offer – Growth strategy framework](https://www.roadtooffer.com/blog/growth-strategy-cases), [CaseStar – Growth strategy](https://www.casestar.io/guides/growth-strategy)

## What the case is asking

Clarify first: growth of *what* — revenue, profit, share — in *what* timeframe, against *what* current base? Two reframes that win the case in the first minute:

- "Doubling $500M revenue in 5 years is ~15%/year while the market grows 2% — so most growth must come from share gains or new lines." (Frames the entire problem.)
- The same math tells you the **growth gap**: target minus the passive base (base compounded at market growth) = what active levers must deliver. Quantify the gap before listing levers.

## The core framework

Split levers into **organic vs inorganic**, and inside organic split **volume vs price**:

```
Organic
├── Sell more to existing customers (cross-sell, upsell, frequency, retention/churn)
├── Acquire new customers (new segments, new channels, marketing)
├── New products / services
├── New geographies
├── New channels
└── Price & mix (price increases, mix shift to premium)
Inorganic
├── Acquisition
├── Joint venture
└── Partnership / licensing
```

Revenue decomposes cleanly as **Revenue = Volume × Price**, and Volume = Customers × Frequency × Units per purchase — "sell to more people" vs "sell more often" vs "sell more per visit."

**The Ansoff Matrix** is a useful secondary lens (penetration / product development / market development / diversification) to make sure your structure doesn't miss a product-market combination — but don't recite it as your whole framework; it ignores inorganic growth and price.

## Step-by-step approach

1. **Clarify objective and current state.** Growth of what, of how much, by when, from what base? Where does current revenue come from (segments, products, channels)?
2. **Size the gap.** Passive base vs target. This tells you how ambitious the levers must be.
3. **Enumerate levers** across the tree above — tailored to the industry. SaaS: new logos, expansion revenue (seats/tiers/modules), churn reduction → ARR = customers × ARPA. Retail: same-store sales (traffic × conversion × basket), new stores, e-commerce, category extension. B2B services: clients, engagement size, utilization, new service lines.
4. **Evaluate and prioritize each lever** on impact, ease of implementation, cost, timing, and risk — score them, don't list them. An impact×feasibility table is the output interviewers reward most.
5. **Quantify the top levers** — size each in revenue terms with stated assumptions, and check whether the stacked levers close the gap.
6. **Recommend a phased plan** — sequence by risk/payback: core quick wins first, expansion second, inorganic last — with the numbers summing to (or intentionally short of) the target, and a rationale for the phasing.

Rule of thumb: **prefer organic first** (cheaper, more sustainable, less integration risk) and turn to inorganic only to close the remaining gap or when speed/capability demand it.

## Key factors and questions to consider

- **Growth is not always good growth** — some revenue destroys margin or ties up capital; state the quality-of-growth criteria.
- **Retention compounds** — in subscription businesses, expansion revenue from existing accounts is usually the cheapest growth: size net revenue retention first.
- **Capability honesty** — can the client execute the lever, or is "go acquire" a confession of weakness?
- **Market context** — if the market is flat, growth must be share or adjacency; if it's growing, standing still costs share.
- **Adjacent vs diversified** — the further from the core, the higher the failure rate; price that risk into your prioritization.
- **Competitive response** — big growth moves invite reaction.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Jumping to growth ideas before diagnosing** what is constraining growth — the most common failure. Diagnose (why hasn't it grown?) before prescribing.
2. **Listing levers without scoring** — "we could enter Europe or launch a loyalty program" is a brainstorm, not prioritized advice.
3. **Unrealistic numbers** — stacked levers that quadruple the market share of conviction; sanity-check every estimate.
4. **Recommending acquisition first** — the most expensive, riskiest lever as a default.
5. **Treating organic vs inorganic as the entire, mandatory frame** — several PrepLounge coaches warn this flags memorization when the case context points elsewhere; tailor.
6. **Ignoring price/mix** — growth is not only volume.

## Worked example (condensed)

**Prompt:** A $500M regional grocery chain needs to double revenue in 5 years (weekly-format case).

**Gap math:** 2× in 5 years ≈ 15%/year. Market grows ~2%, so the passive base reaches ~$552M in 5 years; the active levers must deliver ~$448M total by year 5. Same-store tweaks alone can't do that.

**Levers quantified (assumptions stated):** same-store initiatives (traffic, prepared foods, private label) +$80M/yr by year 5; geographic expansion to 2 adjacent states using existing supply chain +$150M; acquiring a 40-store regional player +$180M; e-commerce/click-and-collect +$40M.

**Recommendation:** "Phase 1: same-store growth plus private label ($80M, lowest risk, 12-month payback). Phase 2: state expansion ($150M, uses existing supply chain, years 2–3). Phase 3: acquisition of a regional player ($180M, years 3–4) — the market is fragmented and acquisition is the only lever that gets us to the target on time; alternatives are slower. E-commerce is a supporting channel, not the headline. Total reaches ~$1B − risks: integration, capital constraints — I'd run debt-capacity analysis before committing."

## Practice prompts

- Coca-Cola asks: how can we return to growth after five flat years?
- A luxury hotel chain wants 20% revenue growth in 3 years. How?
- A B2B SaaS with high NRR but slowing new logos: growth plan?
- A national bank wants to double fee income. Structure the levers.
- A family-owned furniture maker wants to grow without losing control (no equity partners). How?
