# Mergers & Acquisitions Cases

M&A cases ask you to evaluate whether a company should acquire or merge with another — or occasionally sell (divestiture flips the same logic: is the business worth more to someone else than to us?). They appear in roughly 15–20% of MBB second-round interviews, and more often at Bain given its private-equity work. They are high-stakes cases: practice the synergy math until it's automatic.

Source: [Road to Offer – M&A framework](https://www.roadtooffer.com/blog/m-and-a-case-framework), [HackingTheCaseInterview – M&A guide](https://www.hackingthecaseinterview.com/pages/merger-acquisition-case-interview), [CaseStar – M&A cases](https://www.casestar.io/guides/ma-cases)

## What the case is asking

There is no single universal M&A framework — the client's objective determines the structure (StrategyCase identifies five archetypes: target search, deal evaluation, valuation-heavy, strategic rationale, and post-merger integration). But the overwhelming majority test the same core: **rationale → target → synergies → price/returns → go/no-go**.

## The core framework (5 components)

| Component | Key questions |
| --- | --- |
| **Market attractiveness** | Is the target's market large, growing, profitable? How intense is competition? |
| **Company attractiveness** | Is the target a strong performer? Differentiation, moat, growth, margins? |
| **Synergies** | What cost and revenue synergies, in dollars? What one-time integration costs? |
| **Financial implications** | Is the price fair vs valuation methods? Payback period? ROI? |
| **Risks & alternatives** | Integration risk, culture, antitrust, key talent loss. Are build/partner options better? |

## Step-by-step approach

1. **Understand the rationale.** Why does the acquirer want this deal — growth, cost, capability, market access, defense, or financial return? If the strategic rationale is weak, the valuation analysis doesn't matter. State the rationale in one sentence.
2. **Quantify the goal.** Translate the goal into a number: what revenue/margin/share or return target must the deal deliver? This anchors everything.
3. **Test build vs buy vs partner FIRST.** Interviewers specifically probe whether you default to the most expensive option. Can organic growth or a JV deliver the same result cheaper and slower but safer?
4. **Analyze the target.** Market and company attractiveness as above; get the fundamentals before believing the story.
5. **Quantify synergies.** See the math below. Cost synergies are 60–80% of typical deal value and far more reliable than revenue synergies — say so explicitly.
6. **Value the target** with one or more of the three methods below and compare with the asking price. Compute the payback.
7. **Recommend** go/no-go with a price ceiling, top risks, and next steps (due diligence, antitrust check, integration planning).

## Synergy math (drill this)

**Payback period** = (Acquisition premium + Integration costs) ÷ Annual net synergies

Worked: $600M price vs $450M standalone value → $150M premium + $40M integration costs = $190M. At $50M annual net synergies, payback = 3.8 years. Most acquirers want under 3–5 years.

**Cost synergy examples:** procurement consolidation, HQ/back-office elimination, facility rationalization, cross-selling admin reduction. **Revenue synergies:** cross-selling to each other's customers, geographic coverage extension, product bundling, pricing power — treat with explicit skepticism and discount them into the base case. (McKinsey research: over 60% of deals miss synergy targets.)

## Valuation: three simplified methods

1. **Comparable company multiples** — apply the median EV/EBITDA of 3–5 public peers: peers trade 8–10x, target EBITDA $50M → $400–500M.
2. **Precedent transactions** — what acquirers actually paid for similar targets; typically higher because they include a 20–40% control premium.
3. **Simplified DCF logic** — value = free cash flow ÷ (discount rate − growth): $40M FCF growing 3% at 10% discount → $40M/(0.07) ≈ $571M.

Memorize the three logics, not formulas — cases give you data piece by piece.

## Key factors and questions to consider

- **Strategic rationale first, price later** — deals that don't close a real strategic gap fail regardless of price.
- **Synergy phasing and achievability** — synergies accrue over years and frequently miss targets; discount them.
- **Integration risk** — culture, systems, talent flight; the #1 real-world deal killer.
- **Antitrust** — if two large competitors combine, proactively flag share thresholds and possible forced divestitures.
- **Alternative targets** — "this deal" vs "this deal vs other targets/organic options."
- **PE vs corporate lens** — if the acquirer is a PE fund, the objective is IRR/MOIC and the exit matters (see [PE due diligence](/extended/pe-due-diligence)).

## Common pitfalls

1. **Jumping straight to acquire without testing alternatives** (build/partner).
2. **Vague synergy claims** — "there'd be cost synergies" with no numbers is not analysis; always quantify.
3. **Treasuring revenue synergies** — real interviewers teach: discount them, lead with cost synergies.
4. **Ignoring the premium** — the deal must create value *above* the premium paid, not just be a good company.
5. **Skipping integration risk and antitrust** — a no-go risk hiding in plain sight.
6. **Forgetting the recommendation** — a price range with no go/no-go decision is a non-answer.

## Worked example (condensed)

**Prompt:** MedCore ($400M revenue healthcare software) considers acquiring DataPulse ($60M revenue analytics vendor, growing 15%, 25% EBITDA margins). Should they, and at what price?

**Rationale:** MedCore sells EHR software; hospitals increasingly demand analytics. DataPulse adds a capability MedCore's customers want cross-sold. Rationale holds.

**Synergies:** Cost — consolidate overlapping G&A and some engineering: ~$2M/year. Revenue — cross-sell DataPulse into MedCore's 800 hospital clients; conservatively 10% attach at $30K ACV ≈ $2.4M. Total ~$4.4M/year; discount revenue synergies by 50% → ~$3.2M risked.

**Valuation:** Peers trade 8–10x EV/EBITDA; DataPulse EBITDA = 25% × $60M = $15M → $120–150M trading value. Precedent deals with ~35% control premium → $160–200M.

**Payback:** At a $180M price vs ~$135M standalone = $45M premium + $10M integration = $55M ÷ $3.2M ≈ 17 years → clearly over-priced at precedent levels.

**Recommendation:** "I recommend against acquiring at precedent premiums. The strategic rationale is sound, but the deal only works at a price near standalone value (~$150M max) given modest, prisine-scaled synergies. Alternative: partner/invest relationship or a structured earn-out. Next step: due diligence on DataPulse's retention and integration burden."

## Practice prompts

- A retailer considers buying an e-commerce platform to move online. Assess.
- A pharma giant considers acquiring a biotech with one promising pipeline drug.
- A PE fund evaluates buying a packaging company to improve operations and exit in 5 years.
- Your client considers a merger of equals with a direct competitor — evaluate.
- Divestiture variant: should a conglomerate sell its underperforming division?
