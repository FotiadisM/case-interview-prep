# New Product Cases

A new product case asks whether to launch a new product or service — and often how to bring it to market. It is a sibling of market entry (a launch *is* an entry into a new product-market), but the emphasis shifts to **customer need, product-market fit, and go-to-market execution**. In 2026 practice, two flavors dominate: go/no-go evaluations and launch design ("we've decided — how?").

Source: [HackingTheCaseInterview – New product guide](https://www.hackingthecaseinterview.com/pages/new-product-case-interview), [Road to Offer – Product launch](https://www.roadtooffer.com/blog/new-product-launch-case-interview), [CaseBasix – New product guide](https://www.casebasix.com/pages/new-product-case-interview-guide)

## What the case is asking

First, identify the objective from the prompt (Road to Offer):

| Objective | First decision | Structure emphasis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Go / no-go | Should the client launch at all? | Market attractiveness, right to win, economics, risks |
| Launch design | How should an approved product reach the market? | Segment, value proposition, pricing, channels, milestones |
| Failed-launch recovery | Why did the launch miss? | Demand, product-market fit, pricing, channel, execution |

The trap: treating "launch" as "enter the market." If the client has already decided to launch, don't re-answer the go/no-go question.

## The core framework

**New product decision gates** (go/no-go flavor) — four statements that should all hold:

1. The product targets an **attractive market segment** (size, growth, willingness to pay).
2. The product **meets a real customer need and beats competing offerings** (product-market fit, differentiation).
3. The company **can execute the launch** (brand fit, channels, capabilities, time-to-market).
4. The launch **will be profitable** (unit economics, cannibalization-aware).

**Launch-design flavor** — the 5-step go-to-market chain:
1. **Market sizing** — TAM/SAM and realistic year-1 penetration, top-down and bottom-up cross-checked.
2. **Customer segmentation** — 2–3 segments by willingness to pay, use case, acquisition channel; pick the beachhead segment (highest value ÷ lowest acquisition cost).
3. **Pricing** — value-based with competitive anchor and cost floor; a 3-tier structure and a recommended tier (see [Pricing](/core/pricing)).
4. **Distribution & channels** — direct/retail/digital/partnerships by where the segment shops; channel economics (margin after channel fees, CAC).
5. **Success metrics** — 3–4 KPIs with numeric targets at 6 and 12 months: adoption (market share, CAC), engagement (retention, NPS), financial (revenue, LTV/CAC).

**Underlying business-situation lenses** (used to structure, never recited whole): the 5Cs (Customers, Competitors, Company, Collaborators, Context), STP (Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning), and the 4Ps for execution detail.

## Step-by-step approach

1. **Understand and restate** — what's the product, who's the client, what does success look like (launch revenue target? share? strategic entry?).
2. **Clarify the objective** — go/no-go, launch design, or recovery — in one sentence, confirmed with the interviewer.
3. **Structure** — the relevant gates or the 5-step chain, tailored, with a hypothesis ("My hypothesis is a launch makes sense in the fitness-enthusiast segment but not mass market").
4. **Analyze** — size the market, segment customers, test need/differentiation (interviews, analogues, willingness to pay), assess capability fit and cannibalization, then build unit economics: price × units − variable cost − launch investment → breakeven timing.
5. **Recommend** — decision (launch/pilot/no), beachhead segment, price architecture, channel plan, KPIs with targets, top risks (adoption risk, cannibalization, competitor response), and staged next steps.

## Key factors and considerations

- **Cannibalization is the hidden question** — will the new product steal higher-margin sales of existing lines? Model it explicitly.
- **Beachhead logic** — launching to one segment first is almost always the right answer; mass-market all-at-once launches are the tell of a junior.
- **Channel economics** — a product can be great at 60% gross margin and dead after retail's cut; compute the landed margin.
- **Product-market fit evidence** — surveys say what people say; analogues and funnel conversion data say what they do; ask for evidence, not claims.
- **Time-to-market** — moats decay; a 2028 launch against a 2026 need is a competitor's gift.
- **Price architecture** — capture willingness-to-pay differences with tiers, not one price.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Treating launch design as go/no-go** (or vice versa) — misreading the objective.
2. **Spending 60–70% of the case on market sizing** and skipping pricing/channels/metrics — the classic new-product failure mode.
3. **Single-segment thinking** — one generic customer profile misses segmentation, the highest-value differentiator in this case type.
4. **Cost-plus pricing as default** — the weakest pricing logic; lead with value.
5. **Ignoring cannibalization and channel costs** — the two places the economics quietly break.
6. **Ending with a launch plan but no KPIs** — how do we know in 6 months whether it worked?

## Worked example (condensed)

**Prompt:** A beverage giant considers launching an energy drink. They have distribution and brand. Should they launch?

**Structure (go/no-go):** segment attractiveness → need & differentiation → execution capability → economics.

**Analysis:** Energy drink market: $20B, growing 8%/yr, concentrated (top 2 hold 60% share). Need: verified — functional-energy occasions are growing; whitespace exists in natural/low-sugar position. Differentiation: our formulation is genuinely lower-sugar — credible with tests. Capability: full distribution network, strong retailer relationships; brand stretch risk from our "family" image — needs a sub-brand. Economics: at $2.20/can, 55% gross margin, launch+marketing $80M and 3% share by year 3 → breakeven mid-year-2, but ~30% of volume would cannibalize our sports drink at similar margins → NPV still positive but modest.

**Recommendation:** "Launch as a sub-brand positioned on natural energy, focusing on the 18–35 urban segment first, priced at $2.50 premium to reflect differentiation. Economics break even in year 2 before cannibalization, and remain positive after — but I'd only proceed if the category research confirms our low-sugar claim matters to buyers; risk is failing to convert shelf space into repeat trial, so success gate = 25% repeat rate by month 6."

## Practice prompts

- A tech company considers a fitness tracker line. Go or no-go?
- A bank wants to launch a banking app for teenagers. How?
- A coffee chain considers prepared food. Launch design, please.
- Your client launched a DTC skincare brand; sales are 40% below plan. Recover.
- A car maker considers subscription-based driver-assist features. Structure.
