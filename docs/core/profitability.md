# Profitability Cases

**The most common case type in consulting interviews.** Profitability drives roughly 30–40% of first-round cases across McKinsey, BCG, and Bain. It is also the building block of nearly every other case type — market entry economics, M&A synergies, and pricing volume trade-offs all lean on the same revenue-cost logic.

Source: [HackingTheCaseInterview – Profitability guide](https://www.hackingthecaseinterview.com/pages/profitability-case-interview), [Management Consulted – Case interview guide](https://managementconsulted.com/case-interview/)

## What the case is asking

A company's profits (or margins) are declining, or profitability is below target — sometimes hidden inside a framing like "our client is worried about its performance" or "a PE fund just acquired this company and profits fell 30%."

**Your job has two phases:**
1. **Quantitatively** — find what is driving the decline (revenue side or cost side, then which specific line within it).
2. **Qualitatively** — explain why that driver is happening (competition, mix, operational failures, market shifts).

## The core framework

```
Profit = Revenue − Costs
Revenue = Price × Quantity
  ├── Price: pricing policy, discounts, mix shift
  └── Quantity: volume per customer × number of customers × share
Costs = Fixed + Variable
  ├── Variable: materials, labor, shipping, transaction costs…
  └── Fixed: rent, overhead, marketing, admin…
```

Adapt it. Rename buckets in the client's language, and segment early and often: by product line, customer segment, geography, or channel. **The single biggest driver of failure is staying at the aggregate level** — total revenue can hold flat while profits collapse because the mix shifted to low-margin products.

## Step-by-step approach

1. **Restate and scope.** Confirm the object of the case: absolute profit, margin percentage, or profitability of a specific unit? Over what period? Profitability (a ratio like margin) can fall while absolute profit rises — clarify which they mean.
2. **Structure.** Present the profit identity above, customized, with a stated hypothesis ("My hypothesis is this is a cost-side problem given the prompt mentions stable demand").
3. **Isolate the side.** Ask for high-level revenue and cost changes first, then prioritize the bigger mover. If revenue fell $80M and costs rose $20M, go after revenue.
4. **Drill the chosen side.** Revenue problem → segment by price vs quantity, then by product/segment/geography. Cost problem → fixed vs variable, then cost category. Request the data that tests your hypothesis; quantify every driver in dollars.
5. **Find the root cause.** Behind the number, ask why: new competitor undercutting price? supplier cost inflation? sales mix degradation? lost key accounts? capacity underutilization making fixed costs heavy?
6. **Synthesize.** Answer first: "Margins fell from 18% to 3% because fuel costs rose 62% while ticket prices stayed flat — a cost-side, commodity-driven squeeze. I recommend X." Back with numbers, name risks, give next steps (e.g., hedging, fuel-efficient fleet renewal, pricing actions).

## Key factors and questions to consider

- **Segment before concluding.** Revenue by product line, costs by category — the aggregate view lies.
- **Mix effects.** Is the company selling more low-margin product? Check the sales mix before blaming price.
- **Price vs volume.** Which moved? They have very different causes and very different fixes.
- **Fixed vs variable.** Rising fixed costs → capacity/utilization problem; rising variable costs → purchasing/supplier/input problem.
- **Benchmarks.** Are margins below industry norm, or is the whole industry squeezed? The fix differs.
- **Quick data asks:** revenue trend by segment, price index, volume in units, cost breakdown (COGS, labor, SG&A), competitor pricing, capacity utilization.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Staying at aggregate level** — the #1 failure. Always demand a breakdown.
2. **Reciting a generic tree** — a textbook profit tree with no customization reads as memorization.
3. **Jumping to solutions** — hearing "declining profits" and immediately proposing cost cuts skips the diagnosis. Interviewers test whether you diagnose before you prescribe.
4. **Ignoring sales mix** — as above.
5. **Treating it as purely qualitative** — every driver must be quantified in dollars; "competition is intense" is not analysis.
6. **Starting with costs by default** — when you have no data yet, revenue is usually the better opening side because revenue problems are more common; but let the data decide.

## Worked example (condensed)

**Prompt:** A US airline's operating margin fell from 18% to 3% in two years while revenue grew 8%.

**Structuring:** "I'll decompose profit into revenue and cost. Since revenue grew 8% but margin collapsed, my early hypothesis is a cost-side problem. I'd structure costs into fuel, labor, maintenance, and airport fees."

**Analysis:** Revenue grew on volume (+5%) and price (+3%) — revenue side looks healthy. Costs: fuel prices rose 62% year over year on an unhedged position, and jet fuel is now 40% of operating cost vs 28% before.

**Quantify:** At $10B revenue, fuel went from $2.8B to ~$4.0B — a $1.2B cost increase, which alone explains ~12 of the 15 margin points lost.

**Recommendation:** "The decline is a concentrated cost-side, external shock: unhedged fuel exposure. I recommend (1) near-term fuel hedging to cap exposure, (2) pricing actions on routes where demand elasticity allows, and (3) network review to retire money-losing routes. Biggest risk: price increases accelerate share loss to low-cost carriers; I'd validate elasticity before implementing."

## Practice prompts

- An electric car maker's profits fell 25% while unit sales rose. What's happening?
- A food delivery platform has 10M monthly users but burns $50M/quarter. How does it get to profitability?
- A private equity firm's packaging company's profit fell 30% since acquisition. What went wrong?
- An e-commerce retailer's shipping costs rose 40% in a year. Raise prices, absorb, or something else?
- A global bank's wealth-management margin shrank from 35% to 22%. Diagnose.

Real worked versions: McKinsey's official practice cases include profitability classics; the included [MBA casebooks](/case-libraries) (Kellogg, Sloan, Tuck, Yale) contain dozens with full solutions.
