# Pricing Cases

Pricing cases test whether you can treat price as a strategic lever. Three question formats: (1) set a price for a new product, (2) evaluate a price change, (3) respond to a competitor's pricing move. The dominant mistake: treating "three pricing strategies" as an answer. The strongest answer **triangulates** cost, competition, and value into a price band, positioned by the client's objective.

Source: [StrategyCase – Pricing case](https://strategycase.com/pricing-case-interview/), [HackingTheCaseInterview – Pricing guide](https://www.hackingthecaseinterview.com/pages/pricing-case-interview), [Road to Offer – Pricing strategy](https://www.roadtooffer.com/blog/pricing-strategy-cases)

## What the case is asking

The single most important 30 seconds: **clarify the objective before structuring.** Profit maximization, revenue growth, market share, or premium positioning each produce a different "right" price for the same product. Also clarify: new or existing product? What do we already know about costs, competitors, and willingness to pay?

## The core framework: the pricing triangle

| Method | What it sets | Strength | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cost-based** | The **floor** | Simple, protects margin | Ignores customer value and demand |
| **Competition-based** | The **market anchor** | Grounded in reality | Assumes rivals priced well |
| **Value-based** | The **ceiling** | Captures true willingness to pay | Hardest to quantify |

Your recommended price lives inside the band, positioned according to the objective. Elasticity (how demand changes with price) then tunes the final number, and a price-move case needs a full elasticity and profit trade-off calculation.

## Step-by-step approach

1. **Clarify the objective** — profit, revenue, share, positioning — and the product/customer context.
2. **Set the floor** — fully-loaded unit cost (variable + allocated fixed), plus the minimum margin the client needs. Price below cost is usually the fastest wrong answer.
3. **Set the anchor** — map the competitive set: rival price points, feature/brand positioning, and where the client sits. For commodities, market price is the answer and your analysis shifts to cost position.
4. **Set the ceiling** — estimate willingness to pay: (a) identify the next-best alternative, (b) quantify the incremental value your product delivers over it (savings, revenue uplift, time, status), (c) price below full value so customers capture some of it and switch.
5. **Weigh the approaches** — which method dominates given the objective: differentiated B2B with measurable ROI → value-led; crowded consumer market → competition-led; utilities/commodities → cost-led.
6. **Build the number** and pressure-test with elasticity: if price rises x%, volume falls by what %, and does profit rise or fall? Segment and tier if willingness to pay varies.
7. **Consider the game** — will competitors undercut, match, or hold? Can they? Include execution: rollout, segmentation fences (e.g., student plans, regional pricing), and the stop signal (when to pull the price).

## Key math to master

- **Price elasticity**: % change in volume ÷ % change in price. Elastic (|E| > 1) → price-sensitive; inelastic (&lt; 1) → room to raise.
- **Profit impact of a price change** — model it explicitly: new volume × (new price − unit cost) vs old volume × (old price − unit cost).
- **Willingness-to-pay estimation** — list tangible and intangible benefits, assign a dollar value each, sum to the ceiling. State every assumption out loud.
- **Reference points worth knowing:** a 1% price increase improves operating profit by roughly 8% for the average S&P company (McKinsey research cited by Road to Offer) — the reason pricing is worth so much interview attention.

## Key factors and questions to consider

- **Differentiated vs commodity** — commodities are price takers: focus on market dynamics and your cost position, not WTP frameworks.
- **Willingness to pay varies** — open the door to tiered or segmented pricing (versions, bundles, discounts by channel).
- **Substitutes define the ceiling** — willingness to pay is anchored to the next best alternative, not the product's features.
- **Strategic context** — a premium price is only durable if the brand or moat supports it; confusing "premium brand" with "pricing power" is a classic fail.
- **Elasticity data absent?** Estimate from analogues, segment sensitivity, and switching costs; never skip the trade-off discussion.
- **Competitive response** — a price rivals can instantly undercut (and a cost structure that lets them) turns a "win" into a margin war.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Reciting the three strategies as the answer** — tools, not structure.
2. **Skipping the objective** — sends the analysis in the wrong direction.
3. **Anchoring on cost first** — cost is the floor, not the starting insight; value should lead for differentiated products.
4. **Ignoring willingness to pay** — the single biggest pricing driver.
5. **Jumping to calculations too early** — precise answers to the wrong question.
6. **Forgetting competitive response and execution** — pricing lives in a game, and a price with no segmentation/rollout plan is half-finished.
7. **Staying purely qualitative** — every pricing case expects the numbers.

## Worked example (condensed)

**Prompt:** A B2B software firm launches a tool that saves a mid-size customer ~$250/month in labor per user. It costs $7/user/month to serve. Comparable tools sell at $15–25/user/month.

**Floor:** $7 cost → price must exceed ~$10 with margin targets. **Anchor:** competitive band $15–25. **Ceiling:** next-best alternative creates $X value; our incremental value ≈ $250/user/month saved — capturing even 10% of it implies a $25 ceiling that actually *matches* competitors rather than exceeding them — so the real question is whether we can price *above* the band on proof of ROI with usage-linked pricing.

**Recommendation:** "Price at $19/user/month for the standard tier — above our $10 floor, competitive with the $15–25 band, and well below the $250/user/month value ceiling, giving clear ROI. Use a 3-tier structure ($19 core, $35 premium for enterprise features) with annual discounting to reduce churn, and a free tier to build adoption. Inelastic customers (core tools) can eventually absorb more; I'd test a price increase on the enterprise tier after 12 months and watch gross churn as the stop signal."

## Practice prompts

- A smartphone maker must price its new flagship. Structure the decision.
- A museum considers raising ticket prices from $20 to $25. Evaluate.
- A drug maker must price a new therapy with clear clinical superiority. How?
- A competitor cut prices 30% in your core market. How do you respond?
- A sports team prices stadium naming rights. What should they bid/charge? (See the PrepLounge "Stadium Naming Rights" case for a full example.)
